Digestive & Liver Health

Fatty liver means extra fat has built up inside the liver cells. It is very common and is often found by chance on an ultrasound. It is frequently linked with excess weight, diabetes, high cholesterol and alcohol use — today it is often called metabolic (dysfunction-associated) liver disease.

Most people have no symptoms. In some, fat leads to inflammation and, over years, scarring (fibrosis). That is why a fatty liver report deserves a proper assessment — blood tests and, where useful, a liver stiffness scan such as Fibrotouch — rather than being ignored.

The good news: in its early stages, fatty liver often improves with weight loss, regular physical activity, diet changes and control of sugar and cholesterol.

Occasional acidity after a heavy or spicy meal is common. It is worth seeing a specialist when acidity is frequent, keeps coming back after stopping medicines, or is accompanied by any of these warning signs:

  • Difficulty or pain while swallowing
  • Vomiting, especially vomiting blood
  • Black stools or anaemia
  • Unexplained weight loss or loss of appetite
  • New symptoms starting later in life

In these situations an upper GI endoscopy may be advised to look directly at the food pipe, stomach and duodenum.

Liver function tests (LFTs) measure enzymes and substances such as SGOT/SGPT, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin and albumin. A raised value tells us the liver is under some stress — it does not by itself tell us why.

Common causes include fatty liver, alcohol, viral hepatitis, certain medicines, gallstones or bile-duct problems. The pattern of the abnormality, your history and further tests help find the cause.

A mildly abnormal report is often manageable, but it should not simply be repeated again and again without an explanation.

A colonoscopy examines the large intestine with a thin flexible camera. It may be advised for:

  • Bleeding from the back passage or blood in the stool
  • A persistent change in bowel habits
  • Unexplained anaemia
  • Chronic diarrhoea
  • Suspected or known inflammatory bowel disease
  • Appropriate screening or surveillance, such as a family history of colon cancer or previous polyps

Polyps found during colonoscopy can often be removed in the same procedure.

They sound alike but are very different conditions.

IBS (Irritable Bowel Syndrome) is a functional disorder — the bowel looks normal but works in an oversensitive way, causing pain, bloating, and constipation and/or diarrhoea. It is common and troublesome, but it does not damage the intestine.

IBD (Inflammatory Bowel Disease) — ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease — involves real inflammation and ulceration of the bowel. It may cause bleeding, persistent diarrhoea, weight loss or anaemia, and needs specialist treatment and monitoring.

Telling them apart sometimes needs blood and stool tests and a colonoscopy.

Gallstones are stone-like deposits that form in the gallbladder. Many people have them without any symptoms, and they are often found on an ultrasound done for another reason.

When they cause problems, they may lead to pain in the upper abdomen, inflammation of the gallbladder, jaundice if a stone moves into the bile duct, or pancreatitis.

Whether gallstones need treatment depends on symptoms and complications. Stones in the bile duct can often be removed endoscopically.

Women’s Health

Some irregularity is common in the first few years after periods begin, and again as women approach menopause. Stress, illness, weight change and travel can also affect the cycle.

It is worth a consultation if periods are persistently unpredictable, very widely spaced, stop for several months (when not pregnant), or come with heavy bleeding, pain, acne or excess hair growth. Conditions such as PCOS and thyroid problems are common, treatable causes.

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS/PCOD) is a common hormonal and metabolic condition. It can cause irregular periods, difficulty with ovulation, acne, increased facial or body hair and weight-related problems.

It looks different in every woman, so management is individualised — lifestyle measures, treatment of menstrual or hormonal problems, support when planning pregnancy, and attention to long-term metabolic health.

Bleeding may be heavier than it should be if you:

  • Need to change pads every one to two hours
  • Pass large clots
  • Bleed for more than about seven days
  • Feel tired, breathless or dizzy, or have been told you are anaemic
  • Find your periods disrupt work, school or daily life

Heavy bleeding is common but should not be accepted as “normal”. Causes such as fibroids, hormonal imbalance or thyroid problems can be evaluated and treated.

Many ovarian cysts are simple, functional cysts linked to the menstrual cycle and disappear on their own. A cyst should be reviewed by a gynaecologist when it is found on a scan, causes pelvic pain or pressure, persists over time, or appears after menopause.

Sudden severe pelvic pain, especially with vomiting or fainting, needs urgent medical attention.

A pre-pregnancy consultation helps you start pregnancy in the best possible health. It may include:

  • Starting folic acid before conception
  • Reviewing existing conditions such as thyroid disease, diabetes or high blood pressure
  • Checking current medicines for safety in pregnancy
  • Basic blood tests and vaccination advice
  • Guidance on weight, diet and lifestyle

Menopause is the natural end of periods, confirmed after twelve months without a period. The years before it — perimenopause — often bring changing cycles and symptoms such as hot flushes, night sweats, sleep disturbance, mood changes and vaginal dryness.

After menopause, attention to bone health, weight and cardiovascular risk becomes more important. The right approach depends on your age, symptoms and medical history. Any bleeding after menopause should always be checked.
